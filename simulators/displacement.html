Interpret the data

Halogens and halide ions

  • Halogens act as oxidising agents: they gain electrons and are reduced: X2 + 2e− → 2X−
  • Ease of reduction: F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2. Going down the group, atomic radius increases, so an incoming electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded. It is attracted less strongly.
  • It follows that halide ions get easier to oxidise down the group. I− is the strongest reducing agent of the halides.
  • A halogen oxidises the halide ions of any halogen below it in group 17: Cl2 + 2Br− → 2Cl− + Br2
  • K+ or Na+ are spectator ions and are left out of the ionic equation.
  • Br2 and I2 can both look orange-brown in water. Shaking with cyclohexane separates them clearly: orange-red for Br2 and purple for I2.
  • F2 is not used in these aqueous tests because it oxidises water itself.

Metals and metal ions

  • The metal atom is oxidised and acts as the reducing agent: M → Mn+ + ne−. The metal ion is reduced and acts as the oxidising agent.
  • A metal displaces (reduces) the ions of any metal that is less easily oxidised than itself.
  • The order of ease of reduction of the ions is the reverse of the order of ease of oxidation of the metals. The ions of the least reactive metal are the most easily reduced.
  • Balance the electrons: Cu + 2Ag+ → Cu2+ + 2Ag. A charge check shows 2+ on each side.
  • Sulfate and nitrate ions are spectators.
Syllabus note: the relative reactivity of metals from displacement reactions does not need to be learned. Exam questions supply the data, and you must interpret it.

Reading a data table: exam technique

  • The metal that reacts with the most ion solutions is the most easily oxidised (strongest reducing agent).
  • The ion that is reduced by the most metals is the most easily reduced (strongest oxidising agent).
  • For the metal, count ticks along its row. For the ion, count ticks down its column.
  • To predict an untested reaction, place both species in your deduced order: a reaction happens only if the metal is above the metal of the ion.

Common errors

  • "Chlorine displaces bromine." At the start, bromide ions are present, not bromine. Better: chlorine oxidises bromide ions to bromine.
  • Mixing up Cl2 (oxidising agent) with Cl− (the product, which is a very weak reducing agent).
  • Writing Cu + Ag+ → Cu2+ + Ag. The atoms balance but the charges and electrons do not.
  • Writing iron as Fe3+ in its reaction with Cu2+(aq). Fe2+ forms, which is why the solution turns pale green.
  • Calling the metal ion "the reducing agent". The species that is reduced is the oxidising agent.
