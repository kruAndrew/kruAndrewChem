Reality check for this complex

    How the model works (and where it simplifies)
    • Every complex is treated as octahedral with six identical donor atoms (three for bidentate en and C₂O₄²⁻), as requested. Real geometry can differ; the reality-check panel flags the main cases.
    • Δo is estimated with Jørgensen's rule, Δo = f(ligand) × g(metal ion), using literature g values from hexaaqua spectra. Jørgensen's rule badly underestimates cyanide complexes (CN⁻ is a strong π-acceptor), so literature Δo values are used for [V(CN)₆]⁴⁻, [Cr(CN)₆]³⁻, [Mn(CN)₆]³⁻, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻/³⁻ and [Co(CN)₆]³⁻; other cyanide combinations use f = 2.0 as an estimate.
    • High spin vs low spin compares Δo with an effective pairing energy for each ion. These are tuned to reproduce the spin states seen in the lab, so they do not match any single textbook table.
    • For one-electron cases (d¹, d⁹, high-spin d⁴ and d⁶) the band sits at Δo, which is the IB picture. For d², d³, d⁷, d⁸ and low-spin d⁶, electron–electron repulsion produces extra bands. Their positions are approximated as fixed multiples of Δo, taken from typical Tanabe–Sugano ratios. That is why Ni²⁺ and Cr³⁺ have more than one band.
    • High-spin d⁵ (Mn²⁺, Fe³⁺) has no spin-allowed d–d transition, so its bands are drawn very weak. In the orbital diagram, no electron can move to eg without flipping its spin.
    • Bands are Gaussian (σ ≈ 1900 cm⁻¹, widened to 3000 cm⁻¹ for the Jahn–Teller ions Cu²⁺, Cr²⁺ and Mn³⁺, whose real bands are very broad). Real d–d bands are Laporte-forbidden and weak (ε ≈ 1–100 dm³ mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹); intensities here are relative, scaled to represent a concentrated solution. Charge-transfer bands are not modelled.
    • The colour seen uses the simple IB colour-wheel model, chosen for learning the idea rather than for accuracy. For each absorption band the model uses its peak wavelength; for a band peaking in the infrared or UV it uses the centre of the part that reaches into the visible, takes that colour on the wheel, and shows the complementary colour opposite it. If two bands both absorb strongly and their complementary colours sit next to each other on the wheel, the two are mixed. If little light is absorbed, the colour is shown pale. Real solutions can look somewhat different; the reality-check panel notes the main cases.
    • The colour wheel follows the IB Chemistry data booklet (section 15): same layout and the same boundaries (400, 424, 491, 575, 585, 647, 700 nm). The six waves in the light diagram are these same six bands, each showing the average % of that band transmitted.
